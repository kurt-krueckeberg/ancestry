# Meierrecht and Meierbrief

While *Anerbenrecht* governed the physical inheritance side (how the
property stayed with a single heir), **Meierrecht** was the overarching
tenancy law that defined the legal relationship between the farmer (the
*Meier*) and the landlord (*Gutsherr*).

Here is how this "Meier" system functioned and directly shaped the names
you see in the parish books:

## 1. The Legal Contract: The *Meierbrief*

Under *Meierrecht*, the peasant family did not own the land; they held a
hereditary lease. The landlord issued a formal legal document called a
**Meierbrief** (steward letter).

* The *Meierbrief* was issued to a specific person—the **Meier** (the head of the farm).
* This contract legally anchored the **Hofname** (farm name) to the physical *Stätte*.
* If a new husband married the female heir of a farm, he had to be formally written into a new *Meierbrief* by the landlord. Once his name was on that contract, he legally assumed the role—and consequently, the name—of the *Meier* of that property.

## 2. The "Interim" Loophole

This legal structure is exactly why Philippine Eleonore’s stepfather,
Heinrich Tönnies Werkmeister, kept his birth name.

Under the rules of *Meierrecht*, because he was only a temporary manager
(*Interimswirt*), **the landlord would not issue a new, permanent
*Meierbrief* in Werkmeister's name.** The lease remained legally anchored
to the deceased Blecke's estate. Since Werkmeister was never formally
contracted as the permanent "Meier" of Evesen No. 11, he never legally
assumed the *Hofname* of Blecke.

### 3. The Social Identifier: *Meier* as a Title

In daily village life, the head of a farm was simply referred to as "the
Meier." In many northwestern German villages, the word "Meier" became so
synonymous with "farm manager" that it evolved into a naming practice
itself.

* Neighbors might refer to a man as *Blecken-Meier* (the manager of the Blecke farm).
* Over generations, this linguistic pattern is what caused the word "Meyer" or "Meier" to become one of the most common surnames in Germany, often attaching itself to older farm names.

So, while *Anerbenrecht* decided *who* got the farm, it was **Meierrecht**
and the **Meierbrief** that legally dictated whose name went on the tax
rolls, ultimately driving the fluid naming practices you are untangling in
the Petzen records.

# Meierrecht, Anerbenrecht, Leibzucht, and Leibzuchtshaus in Schaumburg-Lippe

In late eighteenth- and early to mid-nineteenth-century Schaumburg-Lippe, these institutions regulated how a Stätte was held, transferred to a successor, and used to support the previous holder after the transfer.

A Stätte should not automatically be understood as a farm. It was a recognized holding or household establishment. A Stätte could be a substantial agricultural holding, but it could also consist principally of a small dwelling, a garden plot, a few animals, limited land or common rights, and perhaps one or more outbuildings. Its holder might earn most of his living as a shoemaker, tailor, weaver, carpenter, day laborer, or other craftsman rather than through agriculture.

The recognized holder of such a Stätte was commonly called a Kolon. The term described his legal and social relationship to the holding; it did not necessarily identify his principal occupation as farming.

## 1. Meierrecht: the Kolon’s right to hold the Stätte

Under the Meierrecht, the Kolon possessed the recognized right to occupy, use, and transmit the Stätte, while a superior lord or institution retained certain rights over it. In Schaumburg-Lippe, that superior authority was often the territorial ruler or princely administration, although noble estates, ecclesiastical bodies, or other landholders could also possess particular seigneurial rights.

By the late eighteenth century, Meierrecht was generally much more secure than an ordinary temporary rental arrangement. The Kolon and his family normally expected the Stätte to remain in the family, provided that its obligations were fulfilled and the holding was not improperly neglected or alienated.

Transfer to a successor could nevertheless require the consent of the competent authority and formal recognition of the new holder through a Bemeierung, a Meierbrief, or a related administrative procedure. A Meierbrief identified the person recognized as the holder under the applicable tenure arrangement. The existence of such a document could therefore distinguish the legally recognized holder from someone who merely managed or occupied the Stätte in practice. 

A person serving only as an Interimswirt was a temporary manager or acting holder. Such a person might administer the Stätte during a minority, widowhood, disputed succession, or other transitional period without becoming its permanent, formally recognized Kolon. The Meierrecht applied to the recognized holding as a legal unit. On a large Stätte, this unit might include a house, barns, extensive arable land, meadows, livestock, and common rights. On a small craftsman’s Stätte, it might consist primarily of the house, garden, a small amount of land, limited livestock, and rights or obligations connected with the holding or house number.

Thus, Meierrecht in Schaumburg-Lippe did not concern only farms. It governed the hereditary or transmissible possession of Stätten of widely differing sizes and economic character.

## 2. Anerbenrecht: transfer to one principal successor

The succession principle generally described as Anerbenrecht provided that the essential Stätte passed to one principal successor, the Anerbe, rather than being physically divided among all the children.

The Anerbe might be a son or, depending on the family circumstances and applicable local practice, a daughter. A daughter who succeeded to the holding could be described as an Erbtochter.

The exact choice of successor could be affected by customary succession rules, the wishes of the family, the suitability of the proposed successor, marriage arrangements, and the approval of the competent lord or administrative authority. It is therefore preferable not to assume that one rigid rule of eldest-son or youngest-son succession applied uniformly to every Stätte in Schaumburg-Lippe.

The other children were the weichende Erben, the heirs who did not receive the Stätte itself. They might receive money, furniture, clothing, livestock, assistance in establishing another household, or other property that could be separated from the holding.

The settlement provided to a non-succeeding heir was commonly called an Abfindung. Depending on the family’s resources and the character of the Stätte, the Abfindung might consist of money, movable property, a marriage portion, assistance in establishing another household, or continued shelter and support. The uploaded Anerbenrecht discussion correctly identifies Abfindung as an important part of the treatment of co-heirs, although its precise form should not be assumed to have been identical in every case. 

The purpose of Anerbenrecht was to preserve the legal and economic continuity of the recognized holding.

On a substantial agricultural Stätte, this prevented the land and buildings from being divided into progressively smaller pieces. On a small shoemaker’s or tailor’s Stätte, it might preserve only the dwelling, garden, limited land, common rights, and the legal position attached to the holding.

The word “undivided” therefore requires qualification. It does not necessarily mean that every movable object or every item owned by the family passed to the Anerbe. It means principally that the core Stätte and the rights and obligations constituting it continued under one recognized holder.

## 3. Succession and the Hof- or Stättenname

In Schaumburg-Lippe and neighboring parts of northwestern Germany, a person’s identification could be closely connected with the Stätte he or she occupied.

When an Erbtochter succeeded to a Stätte and her husband became its recognized holder, the husband might subsequently be identified in parish, tax, administrative, or local records by the established Hofname or Stättenname rather than exclusively by his birth surname. The Meierbrief or other formal recognition of the new holder could reinforce this association between the person and the holding.  

This should not necessarily be understood as a modern legal surname change in every case. Historical records might use:

* the person’s birth surname;
* the name of the Stätte;
* both names together;
* or different names at different times.

The Hof- or Stättenname could therefore function as a social, occupational, residential, and sometimes administrative identifier.

This distinction is especially important when reconstructing families from Schaumburg-Lippe parish records. A change in the surname used for a man after marriage might indicate that he had entered and assumed a Stätte, rather than that he belonged by birth to the family previously associated with that name.

## 4. Leibzucht: support reserved after the transfer

When the holder transferred the Stätte to a successor, the former holder and spouse—or, in some cases, a surviving widow or widower—could retain lifelong housing and maintenance as a Leibzucht.

The persons entitled to these benefits were called Leibzüchter. They had surrendered possession or active control of the Stätte but retained specified rights against the new holder.

The Leibzucht might include:

* lifelong housing;
* grain, potatoes, bread, milk, meat, or other food;
* firewood or peat;
* the use of a garden or small parcel of land;
* the right to keep a cow, pig, goats, or poultry;
* clothing, linen, or payments of money;
* care during illness and old age;
* and sometimes funeral and burial expenses.

These provisions were not necessarily identical from one Stätte to another. They depended upon the size, property, and productive capacity of the holding.

A large agricultural Stätte might be able to provide a separate dwelling, substantial annual quantities of food and fuel, livestock, and the use of specified land. A small craftsman’s Stätte might provide only a room or small dwelling, garden use, modest provisions, and personal support from the successor.

The Leibzucht was therefore not simply an informal promise that children would care for elderly parents. It was a recognized package of reserved rights, commonly established through a transfer agreement, marriage agreement, succession arrangement, Meier record, or other administrative instrument.

The term could also be used somewhat flexibly. In a particular record, die Leibzucht might refer to:

* the legal entitlement;
* the property or provisions assigned for support;
* the dwelling occupied by the Leibzüchter;
* or the entire secondary establishment connected with those rights.

Its precise meaning must therefore be determined from the context.

## 5. Leibzuchtshaus: the dwelling attached to the Leibzucht

A Leibzuchtshaus was a dwelling used by the person or couple entitled to the Leibzucht.

On a substantial Stätte, it might be a distinct secondary house standing near the principal dwelling and agricultural buildings. When the successor assumed the principal house and control of the holding, the former holder and spouse could move into this smaller dwelling.

On a modest Stätte, however, the Leibzuchtshaus might be a very small structure. In other cases, no separate Leibzuchtshaus existed at all. The Leibzüchter might occupy designated rooms in the principal house or another existing building.

The distinction can therefore be expressed as follows:

Leibzucht was the entire arrangement of lifelong housing and support.

Leibzuchtshaus was a house or separate dwelling supplied as one part of that arrangement.

Historical usage was not always perfectly consistent. A building or secondary establishment might itself occasionally be referred to simply as die Leibzucht.

The plural Leibzuchtshäuser means several such dwellings.

## How the system worked as a whole in Schaumburg-Lippe

A simplified transfer of a Stätte might proceed in this way:

1. A Kolon held a recognized Stätte under the applicable Meier and seigneurial arrangements.

2. One child or other qualified successor was selected to become the Anerbe.

3. If the successor was a daughter, she might be described as the Erbtochter.

4. The proposed succession was recorded or approved by the competent lord or territorial authority.

5. A Bemeierung, Meierbrief, or related record might formally recognize the new holder.

6. The Anerbe became the new Kolon and received the core Stätte as a continuing unit.

7. A temporary Interimswirt, if one had been managing the Stätte during the transition, did not necessarily become its permanent recognized holder.

8. The weichende Erben received whatever Abfindungen the family and holding could provide without dismantling the Stätte.

9. A husband who married an Erbtochter and became the recognized holder might subsequently be identified by the established Hof- or Stättenname.

10. The former holder and spouse—or a surviving widow or widower—reserved a Leibzucht.

11. They occupied a Leibzuchtshaus, designated rooms, or some other accommodation provided by the arrangement.

12. The new Kolon became responsible for delivering the specified housing, food, fuel, care, or other benefits.

The institutions therefore supported different parts of the same system:

Meierrecht defined the Kolon’s hereditary or transmissible right to hold the Stätte and the process by which a new holder could be formally recognized.

Anerbenrecht preserved the essential Stätte by placing it in the hands of one principal successor.

Abfindung provided compensation or support for the weichende Erben who did not receive the Stätte.

The Hof- or Stättenname connected the identity of the recognized holder with the holding and could continue even when succession occurred through an Erbtochter and her husband.

Leibzucht protected the former holder, spouse, widow, or widower after possession had passed to the successor.

Leibzuchtshaus was the separate dwelling provided under that arrangement when the size and resources of the Stätte permitted one.

Most importantly, none of these terms proves that the Stätte was a substantial farm. In Schaumburg-Lippe, the same general framework could apply both to a large agricultural holding and to a small house-and-garden Stätte whose Kolon lived principally from a craft or wage labor.

# Meierrecht, Anerbenrecht, Leibzucht, and Leibzuchtshaus in Schaumburg-Lippe

In Schaumburg-Lippe, these institutions regulated how a **Stätte** was held, transferred to the next generation, and used to support the previous holder after the transfer.

A **Stätte** should not automatically be understood as a farm. It was a recognized holding or household establishment. A Stätte could be a substantial agricultural holding, but it could also consist principally of a small dwelling, a garden plot, a few animals, limited land or common rights, and perhaps one or more outbuildings. Its holder might earn most of his living as a shoemaker, tailor, weaver, carpenter, day laborer, or other craftsman rather than through agriculture.

The recognized holder of such a Stätte was commonly called a **Kolon**. The term described his legal and social relationship to the holding; it did not necessarily identify his principal occupation as farming.

## 1. Meierrecht: the Kolon’s right to hold the Stätte

Under the **Meierrecht**, the Kolon possessed the recognized right to occupy, use, and transmit the Stätte, while a superior lord or institution retained certain rights over it. In Schaumburg-Lippe, that superior authority was often the territorial ruler or princely administration, although noble estates, ecclesiastical bodies, or other landholders could also possess particular seigneurial rights.

By the late eighteenth century, Meierrecht was generally much more secure than an ordinary temporary rental arrangement. The Kolon and his family normally expected the Stätte to remain in the family, provided that its obligations were fulfilled and the holding was not improperly neglected or alienated. Transfer to a successor could nevertheless require the consent of the competent authority and formal recognition of the new holder through a **Bemeierung** or a related administrative procedure.

The Meierrecht applied to the recognized holding as a legal unit. On a large Stätte, this unit might include a house, barns, extensive arable land, meadows, livestock, and common rights. On a small craftsman’s Stätte, it might consist primarily of the house, garden, a small amount of land, limited livestock, and rights or obligations connected with the holding or house number.

Thus, Meierrecht in Schaumburg-Lippe did not concern only farms. It governed the hereditary possession of Stätten of widely differing sizes and economic character. More broadly, Meierrecht had developed into a hereditary right to use another’s property subject to continuing obligations, and by the end of the eighteenth century it could apply across different sizes of holdings.

## 2. Anerbenrecht: transfer to one principal successor

The succession principle generally described as **Anerbenrecht** provided that the essential Stätte passed to one principal successor, the **Anerbe**, rather than being physically divided among all the children.

The Anerbe might be a son or, depending on the family circumstances and applicable local practice, a daughter. The exact choice of successor could be affected by customary succession rules, the wishes of the family, the suitability of the proposed successor, marriage arrangements, and the approval of the competent lord or administrative authority.

The other children were the **weichende Erben**—the heirs who did not receive the Stätte itself. They might receive money, furniture, clothing, livestock, assistance in establishing another household, or other property that could be separated from the holding. The amount they received depended heavily on the resources of the family and the size of the Stätte.

The purpose was to preserve the legal and economic continuity of the recognized holding.

On a substantial agricultural Stätte, Anerbenrecht prevented the farmland and buildings from being divided into progressively smaller pieces. On a small shoemaker’s or tailor’s Stätte, it might preserve only the dwelling, garden, limited land, common rights, and the legal position attached to the holding.

The word **undivided** therefore requires some qualification. It does not necessarily mean that every movable object or every item owned by the family passed to the Anerbe. It means principally that the core Stätte and the rights and obligations constituting it continued under one recognized holder.

It is also preferable not to imagine a single, uniform nineteenth-century statute governing every succession. For the period in question, **Anerbenrecht** describes the prevailing principle of impartible succession, while the precise procedure could depend upon the legal status of the particular Stätte and local or manorial practice.

## 3. Leibzucht: support reserved after the transfer

When the holder transferred the Stätte to a successor, the former holder and spouse—or, in some cases, a surviving widow or widower—could retain lifelong housing and maintenance as a **Leibzucht**.

The persons entitled to these benefits were called **Leibzüchter**. They had surrendered possession or active control of the Stätte but retained specified rights against the new holder.

The Leibzucht might include:

* the lifelong right to occupy a house, rooms, or another part of the Stätte;
* grain, potatoes, bread, milk, meat, or other food;
* firewood or peat;
* the use of a garden or small parcel of land;
* the right to keep a cow, pig, goats, or poultry;
* clothing, linen, or payments of money;
* care during illness and old age;
* and, sometimes, funeral and burial expenses.

These provisions were not necessarily identical from one Stätte to another. They depended upon the size, property, and productive capacity of the holding.

A large agricultural Stätte might be able to provide a separate dwelling, substantial annual quantities of food and fuel, livestock, and the use of specified land. A small craftsman’s Stätte might provide only a room or small dwelling, garden use, modest provisions, and personal support from the successor.

The Leibzucht was therefore not simply an informal promise that children would care for their elderly parents. It was a recognized package of reserved rights, commonly established through a transfer agreement, marriage agreement, succession arrangement, Meier record, or other administrative instrument.

The term could also be used somewhat flexibly. In a particular record, **die Leibzucht** might refer to the legal entitlement, the property or provisions assigned for support, or the physical establishment occupied by the Leibzüchter. Its precise meaning must therefore be determined from the context.

## 4. Leibzuchtshaus: the dwelling attached to the Leibzucht

A **Leibzuchtshaus** was a dwelling used by the person or couple entitled to the Leibzucht.

On a substantial Stätte, it might be a distinct secondary house standing near the principal dwelling and agricultural buildings. When the successor assumed the principal house and control of the holding, the former holder and spouse could move into this smaller dwelling.

On a modest Stätte, however, the Leibzuchtshaus might be a very small structure. In other cases, no separate Leibzuchtshaus existed at all. The Leibzüchter might occupy designated rooms in the principal house or another existing building.

The distinction can therefore be expressed as follows:

* **Leibzucht** was the entire arrangement of lifelong housing and support.
* **Leibzuchtshaus** was a house or separate dwelling supplied as one part of that arrangement.

Nevertheless, historical usage was not always perfectly consistent. A building itself might occasionally be referred to simply as **die Leibzucht**.

The plural **Leibzuchtshäuser** means several such dwellings.

## How the system worked as a whole in Schaumburg-Lippe

A simplified transfer of a Stätte might proceed in this way:

1. A Kolon held a recognized Stätte under the applicable Meier and seigneurial arrangements.
2. One child or other qualified successor was selected to become the Anerbe.
3. The proposed succession was recorded or approved by the competent lord or territorial authority.
4. The Anerbe became the new Kolon and received the core Stätte as a continuing unit.
5. The other heirs received whatever settlements the family and holding could provide without dismantling the Stätte.
6. The former holder and spouse—or a surviving widow or widower—reserved a Leibzucht.
7. They occupied a Leibzuchtshaus, designated rooms, or some other accommodation provided by the arrangement.
8. The new Kolon became responsible for delivering the specified housing, food, fuel, care, or other benefits.

The four institutions therefore supported different parts of the same system:

**Meierrecht** defined the Kolon’s hereditary or transmissible right to hold the Stätte.

**Anerbenrecht** preserved the essential Stätte by placing it in the hands of one principal successor.

**Leibzucht** protected the former holder, spouse, widow, or widower after possession had passed to the successor.

**Leibzuchtshaus** was the separate dwelling provided under that arrangement when the size and resources of the Stätte permitted one.

Most importantly, none of these terms proves that the Stätte was a substantial farm. In Schaumburg-Lippe, the same legal framework could apply both to a large agricultural holding and to a small house-and-garden Stätte whose Kolon lived principally from a craft or from wage labor.
